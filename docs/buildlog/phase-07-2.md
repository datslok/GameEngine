# Build log: Phase 7: Cloth simulation (Oct 24 2026 onwards) (part 2 of 3)

## Phase 7: Cloth simulation (Oct 24 2026 onwards) (part 2 of 3)
- `gpu-test-tolerances` (Oct 25 2026): the three GPU test groups that failed on this machine outside the cloth (seen in `gpu-cloth-solver`) were the tests' tolerances, not the shaders; only tests changed, so the picture cannot have.
  - **`ggxSampleHalfway` and `gaussianSamplePoint`:** each is an amplitude times the cosine or sine of an angle, checked with a relative tolerance. Vulkan allows sin and cos an absolute error, 2^-11 on -pi..pi, so near a zero of the cosine the result may be off by far more than its own size (measured: 1e-4 where the cosine is about 2e-4). A `trigonometricTolerance(amplitude)` in `tests/gpu/gpu_check.h` allows 2^-11 times the amplitude: worst now 21% and 36% of it.
  - **`screenRay step`:** a ray cut back to just in front of the camera divides by a w near `SSR_NEAREST_W`, so its step runs to millions of pixels and rounds to a few tenths of a percent (0.3% of 7e7). The march never uses that much: the test now checks the step's direction on screen (an error in w scales x and y alike) and where the ray stops (start + step x end), as well as its start and end; worst 9% and 5%.
  - **Why they passed before is not known:** the inputs are fixed, so the GPU's rounding changed, probably with a driver update.
  - **Checked:** `build/gputests.exe`: every shader library matches its mirror, no validation errors (the golden images not run).

- `hierarchical-cloth` (Oct 25 2026): the low-frequency layer on the CPU, which turned into Vertex Block Descent with Galerkin multigrid levels: `cloth/cloth_energy`, `cloth/cloth_block_descent`, `cloth/cloth_hierarchy`, `ClothSettings::method` and `hierarchyLevels`. XPBD stays the default until the GPU has the rest.
  - **Explained first; the user chose** the paper's general coarsening (for garments later) over every second row and column, and two branches (the CPU version judged on the yardstick, then the GPU port).
  - **Müller 2008 as written, first.** The paper read in full: its steps re-save each level's positions, so with three or more levels the coarsest pull never reaches the fine points; passing each parent's whole motion down measured about 5 times better. Rest lengths straight across the rest shape, since our grids' paths along the edges are 41% too long. The paper's two parents a point made hierarchies of 7 or more levels shake a settled sheet apart; three parents and no level under 50 points fixed the yardstick (36% to 0.3% at 200 squares).
  - **The user saw the cloth lab "jiggle around a lot".** Reproduced: a sheet pinned flat by its four corners never settles with the levels (3-17 m/s). Slack in the coarse limits, the border kept coarse, and weights passing on a stretch all failed; following a quarter of the parents' motion calmed it, and the user rejected it: "scrap this, lets find the root cause and a better way".
  - **The root cause, measured:** a substep is an implicit Euler step minimising one energy, phi (inertia plus the fabric's energy), and Müller's coarse pass raised phi in every substep measured, moving points by up to 8.7 mm where gravity moves them 3 microns: its limits aim at zero stretch (a different rest than the fabric's, the full approximation scheme's missing tau correction) and move whole regions as if they weighed a point.
  - **Researched** (two agents, papers read): Lu, Yuan, Mo & Hu 2025's Galerkin multigrid (the coarse step built from phi's own gradient, the region's mass in its stiffness), the theory behind it (subspace correction, monotone multigrid's truncation of held points), MGPBD's one-sentence critique of HPBD, Tamstorf 2015, Xian 2019, long-range attachments.
  - **Rebuilt as a Galerkin coarse step with a line search;** each step now lowers phi (to a millionth, float rounding). It still shook next to XPBD: XPBD's multipliers must account for every move from the prediction, and a coarse move it did not make breaks that bookkeeping. With a primal smoother (vertex block descent, which lowers phi itself) on both sides of the coarse step, the hammock settled calmer than XPBD's. So the levels and vertex block descent are one step; the roadmap's two items merged.
  - **Vertex block descent:** each point's exact Newton step on phi with its 3 x 3 block, points coloured so that none sharing an edge or hinge step together, both bending models, the hand in phi, contacts positional after. Alone it stretches as XPBD does.
  - **Measured** (quick yardstick, 15 substeps): path stretch 0.27%, 0.35%, 0.57% at 25, 50, 100 squares, against XPBD's 1.04%, 3.5%, 9.6%; every sheet settled to a millimetre a second. The full table is in `docs/research/CLOTHGPU.md`, Part 5.
  - **The cost, 8-16 times XPBD's on the CPU,** is each level's gradient and line search over the whole cloth, each pass ending at a barrier. Cut from 24 times: the gradient a point at a time (one pass, not a colour each), each level's weights straight from the cloth's points (one pass each way), the line search's terms summed once each. Tried and dropped: additive levels (cheaper, but 3.4% stretch at 100 squares), fewer substeps (5 or 8: 17% or 2.6%), bigger jobs (slower). Skipping the two finest levels saves a third for a little stretch: a knob for later.
  - **A quick yardstick** (`make yardstick-quick`, about a minute), after the user found the full one slowed development down; the full run settles vertex block descent at the fabric's substeps only.
  - **Checked:** the tests (debug and release, tests first: the coarsening on a wheel, a chain and a grid, parents' weights, a sheet's levels, small cloths without levels, pins on every level, P^T as P's transpose, the region masses, phi by hand, the coarse step never raising phi on a busy sheet and a settled hammock, rest and a fold left alone, held points staying, settled sheets stretching under half, the hammock settling calmer than XPBD's, the hand reaching further, threads alike; vertex block descent's colours, free fall, Hooke's law, a sweep lowering phi, a hanging sheet, the hinge model, threads alike; the quick option) and the quick and full yardsticks on the release build. Left to the user: the cloth lab by eye, with Cloth on GPU off, Solver set to Vertex block descent and Coarse levels up.

- `gpu-block-descent` (Oct 25 2026): vertex block descent on the GPU, the first half of the low-frequency layer's GPU port: `cloth_block.comp`, the per-point terms in `cloth.glsl` and `cloth_energy`, `VulkanCloth` running whichever solver a tick's settings name. The coarse levels come next, in `gpu-cloth-levels`.
  - **Explained first; the user chose** two branches (the sweep, then the levels), one invocation per point (a small workgroup per point, as Newton does, only if measured), and for the next branch the line search as a reduction on the GPU rather than Lu et al.'s fixed step.
  - **One invocation per point,** because it sums a point's terms in the CPU's order, so it lands nearest the CPU and is the easiest to check, and because a cloth point has only six edges and about eighteen hinge corners: the workgroup version's gain was measured on solids, and what costs a GPU cloth is its dispatches, not its invocations.
  - **The terms named:** `clothPointForce` was split into `addInertiaTerm`, `addEdgeTerm`, `addIsometricHingeTerm`, `addHingeAngleTerm` and `addHandTerm`, each added to the force and block so far, so each has a GLSL twin to test against, and a test checks the sum is `clothPointForce` to the bit. The CPU's results did not change.
  - **What a tick needs:** the prediction (written by the predict pass, which also slides the pins), each point's edge and hinge lists and the points in colour order, uploaded once; the tick's record gains the energy's stiffnesses and the solver (96 bytes). The hand is part of each point's energy, so the finish pass applies it only for XPBD.
  - **Cost:** a dispatch per point colour (4 on a grid, counted in `cloth-gpu-timing`; first written here as about 20), so the sweep records about a quarter of XPBD's dispatches (90 a tick against 330); the yardstick gains a GPU row for it.
  - **Checked:** the tests (debug and release, tests first: each term by hand, a busy sheet's every point equal to its terms summed to the bit with both bending models, the tick's new numbers, the dispatch count); the game, the GPU tests and their shaders build without warnings. Not run, at the user's request: `make gputest` (each term and the block solve against their mirrors, a busy sheet's ticks and a settled sheet with vertex block descent) and the yardstick. The user saw the lab's sheets jiggle with the new solver on the GPU; on the CPU vertex block descent without its levels sways as much as XPBD (measured: rms 7 against 9 mm/s at 28 squares after 10 s, 52 against 37 at 100; with the levels 0.03 and 23), and the user found the lab's sheets act identically with Cloth on GPU on and off: the method, not the port. - `gpu-cloth-levels` (Oct 25 2026): the coarse levels on the GPU, the second half of the low-frequency layer's port: `packClothLevels`, `cloth_levels.glsl` and seven passes (`cloth_held`, `cloth_gradient`, `cloth_coarse_step`, `cloth_spread`, `cloth_line_terms`, `cloth_line_total`, `cloth_line_apply`), the yardstick's "GPU VBD+L" row.
  - **Explained first; the user chose** the levels' buffers in a second descriptor set, the line search's sum in two dispatches (partials, then the total) rather than one with an atomic counter, and the port alone in this branch, its cost cut once measured.
  - **Every level's lists in one buffer a kind,** a 32-byte record per level saying where its own start: a shader cannot take a list of buffers whose length changes from cloth to cloth, and the pass needs only its level's number in its push constants.
  - **The line search is a reduction:** each workgroup sums its terms as a tree in shared memory into one partial, then one workgroup sums the partials the same way and leaves the step length in a buffer the move reads. Fixed order, so the same GPU repeats a run to the bit (atomic adds would add in the order threads arrive). Float, where the CPU sums in double: the curvature's terms are never negative, so the tree's relative error is about 18 times float's precision for 300,000 terms, and a slope that cancels to near zero means the cloth is already at the line's minimum. The CPU never reads the step: reading it back would stall the GPU about 135 times a tick.
  - **The CPU's pieces named for their mirrors:** `addCoarseEdgeTerm`, `inertiaCurvature`, `edgeCurvature`, `isometricHingeCurvature`, `hingeAngleCurvature`, `handCurvature`, `lineSearchStep`; `clothLineSearch` and the coarse step now add them, to the same bits as before (a test checks the step equals its terms summed in a plain loop).
  - **Cost, estimated:** six dispatches a level plus the held points and a second sweep, 71 a substep at 200 squares (10 levels; first written here as 97 with 9, before the point colours were counted), 2.5 ms a tick by the dispatch count; the yardstick's GPU rows will say. The cuts (skipping the two finest levels, the reduction in one pass, the move folded into the next gradient) wait for those numbers.
  - **Checked:** the tests (debug and release, tests first: the curvature terms and the step by hand, the line search equal to its terms, the coarse edge term, the levels' packing round trip, the dispatch count with levels); the game, the GPU tests and their shaders build without warnings. Not run, at the user's request: `make gputest` (the new library functions, a busy sheet's ticks and a settled sheet with the levels against the CPU) and the yardstick.
  - **The user's check:** the big sheet hangs identically on the GPU and the CPU with two levels (its creases where the corners are drawn in are the solver's, not the port's). But the lab ran at about 50 fps: each of its three cloths pays about 55 dispatches a substep with two levels (825 a tick, 1.9 ms), about 6 ms a tick for the three, 70% of the GPU's time at 120 ticks a second, and the readout hid it (the cloth passes are not among its timed passes; the CPU's wait for them showed as RENDER). The cost cuts are what make the levels usable.

- `cloth-point-lanes` (Oct 25 2026): each point's terms summed by 8 invocations of a subgroup in vertex block descent's sweep and the levels' gradient (`cloth_point_lanes.glsl`), and each point's lists holding what its terms need (`packPointEdges`, `packPointHinges`).
  - **Why:** the readout showed the sweep and the gradient at about 8 microseconds a dispatch, four times the passes around them, and two thirds of the cloth's time. A thread a point walked about 26 terms in a chain of reads, each waiting on the last, and a colour's 150 to 2,500 points are far too few threads for the GPU to hide the waits behind: latency, not arithmetic.
  - **Explained first; the user chose** 8 lanes a point (32 would leave most idle) and the faster way to add them up. Subgroup add instructions do not promise their order; a butterfly of shuffles is as fast (registers only, no shared memory or barriers) and still adds in a fixed order, since paired lanes add the same two numbers, so it was used.
  - **Each lane's point from its subgroup's numbering,** not the workgroup's (Vulkan does not promise how a workgroup splits into subgroups), so a point's 8 lanes are neighbours in one subgroup; full subgroups, since their size divides the workgroup's 64. The first lane writes the point, after every lane has read it (the shuffles wait for all of them).
  - **Flattened lists:** an edge entry holds the other point and rest length, a hinge corner entry the hinge's points, weights, rest angle, share and corner, so a term reads one entry and then the points: one read fewer in each chain. The constraint arrays stay for XPBD and the line search.
  - **The GPU now adds a point's terms in another order than the CPU** (each lane's, then the butterfly), so the two agree to rounding, as the tick tests allow (10 microns), not term by term.
  - **Checked:** the tests (debug and release, tests first: the flattened lists against the constraints); the game, the GPU tests and their shaders build without warnings. Not run: `make gputest` (the busy sheet's ticks with both solvers and the levels cover the lanes). Left to the user: F4's cloth line in the lab with Coarse levels 0 and 2, against the readings above.

- `cloth-tethers` (Oct 26 2026): tethers (long-range attachments, Kim, Chentanez & Muller 2012), XPBD with a string from each point to its nearest pins, on the CPU and the GPU (`cloth_tethers`, `cloth_finish.comp`), and the yardstick's `XPBD+T` and `GPU+T` rows.
  - **Why:** with the levels costing 8-17 times XPBD and the dress-sized question still open, the user asked whether to shelve vertex block descent and keep plain XPBD, then whether there was a smarter way to beat stretch. Explained: a local solver carries a correction about one edge a pass, so a hanging sheet's stretch grows like diffusion, about four times a doubling (measured: 3-4); the fixes reach further (coarse levels, global solves), add shortcuts (tethers) or solve fewer points. The user chose to try tethers as the cheap way.
  - **Explained first; the user chose** the straight line at rest for the distance along the cloth, behind one function (`clothRestDistance`), since it is exact for every cloth so far, and exact geodesics over the sewn pattern when a dress needs them (the edges' shortest path is up to 41% too long on our grid, fast marching's 1% error as big as the stretch removed); each point's nearest four pins; hard strings with a slack (no compliance); a checkpoint after the CPU before the GPU port.
  - **The step:** the anchor is pinned, so XPBD's multiplier step with no compliance is the point put back on its sphere, delta x = -C n. Each point's strings touch only that point, so the CPU shares the points among every core with the same bits as one thread, and the GPU needs no pass of its own: the strings sit in the end's pass, between the hand and the bodies as on the CPU, which now writes no pinned point's position (it never changes there, and a tether reads it).
  - **Checkpoint** (quick yardstick, release, 16 threads, 15 substeps): path stretch 0.16%, 0.18%, 0.20% at 25, 50, 100 squares, against XPBD's 1.04%, 3.5%, 9.6% and the levels' 0.27%, 0.35%, 0.57%; worst edge 0.35-1.05% (the levels' 1.2-6.7%, which tethers were not expected to help); sag 1.002 m; settled to 0.02 mm a second; 0.45, 2.1 and 6.8 ms a tick, 12-25% over XPBD and 7-14 times under the levels. The user, in the lab: "looks great, no sag really".
  - **Full yardstick:** the 200-square sheet stretches 0.20% with tethers against XPBD's 36% and the levels' 0.74%, for 17.5 ms a tick on the CPU against 16.6 and 153; on the GPU 0.90 ms, XPBD's own cost, the GPU's rows matching the CPU's to the shown digits on every rung at 15 and 60 substeps (docs/research/CLOTHGPU.md, Part 5). At 60 substeps 0.12-0.18%: the strings already did the work.
  - **On by default,** the user's choice after the full yardstick (`ClothSettings::tethers`): every pinned cloth now hangs at its fabric's stretch. The tests that measure a solver itself (XPBD, vertex block descent, the levels) turn them off (`untetheredSettings`, `tests/cloth_helpers.h`), since the strings would hold every solver at their slack.
  - **What it changes:** for cloth hanging from pins the tethers win outright, so the levels' cost cuts are paused until phase 10's garments show whether cloth resting on a body by contact (which tethers cannot help) needs them. Phase 10 gains the tethers' garment item: geodesics over the pattern and anchors on the body.
  - **Checked:** the tests (debug and release, tests first: the straight distance, no pins no tethers, a sheet's two corners nearest first, the nearest four of six, a point put back onto its sphere and one inside left alone, the same bits on any thread count, pinning rebuilding them, the yardstick under 0.5% at 25 and 50 squares and settled, off doing nothing; the GPU records, the tick's stretch and the dispatch count unchanged); the game, the GPU tests and their shaders build without warnings. Not run: `make gputest` (`clothTether` against `solveTether`, busy sheets' ticks with tethers on either solver, a tethered sheet settled 2 s).

- `two-panel-dress` (Oct 26 2026): the dress sewn from two panels on the mannequin, phase 7's third step: sewing patterns cut into cloth (`cloth_pattern`, `math/delaunay`), stitches and hinges across seams (`cloth_seams`), tethers measured along each piece (`ClothRestPaths`), the dress pinned at its shoulders (`cloth_lab_dress`), and the stitches on the GPU (`cloth_stitch.comp`).
  - **Explained first; the user chose** a general outline mesher (an equilateral lattice and the outline's points, Delaunay, the outside dropped: any outline, so phase 10's pattern editor can use it, and the same stiffness every way) over a grid warped onto each panel; hinges across the seams (their rest shape the two triangles unfolded flat) over seams that fold freely; and all three of the dress yardstick's versions (next branch). Both panels in one cloth, so a stitch is two indices into one points array and everything else (colouring, tethers, grabbing, the GPU's upload) works unchanged.
  - **Delaunay:** Bowyer-Watson, its points along a Hilbert curve so each search walks a few steps. A regular lattice puts four points on one circle, so the circle test ties, exactly or in rounding, which can leave the hole a point fills not wholly visible from it and overlap triangles. Ties count as outside and the hole is trimmed until the point sees all of it, so rounding only picks between equally good triangles. Its tests passed first time, the equilateral lattice included.
  - **The lattice's distance from the outline:** at half a spacing a rectangle's border triangles came down to 23 degrees; at 0.6 to 0.8, 37. 0.6 was kept (30.5 degrees round a notch, 25.9 on the dress's armholes and neckline). The dress at 1 cm: 12,027 points in 57 ms.
  - **The stitch's first rule was wrong:** closing each stitch by at most a step from wherever its points were added a step of speed to them every substep (the pull becomes their velocity), an 1,800 m/s2 acceleration: a test pillow sewn shut slammed together (3.9 cm left of 10 after one tick instead of 9.2) and bounced open. Measured from where they were when the substep began, a stitch closing at the seam speed by itself is left alone.
  - **The dress,** sized from the mannequin's own model (cross-sections of its vertices: chest 94 cm round, the arms 5-8 cm off the body at the armpit in its A-pose, the shoulders' tops at 1.44 m): a sleeveless A-line front and back, wrapped round it on a cylinder before sewing, weightless for a second while its seams close. Started with the straps 1-4 cm over the shoulders, a seam's straight pull ran through the rounded shoulder: one strap was pushed off sideways and sewn shut in the armpit at 1.20 m, the other caught 21 cm open. Started 10 cm over them, the seams close in the air and the dress drops onto the shoulders.
  - **Tethered to the straps,** the user's request after seeing it hang by contact ("the straps need to be tethers to the shoulders"). Explained and chosen: the shoulder stitches' points pinned (as a dress is pinned on a dress form) and carried by the lab across, then down onto the shoulders while the dress sews (pins from the start, since GPU cloth takes its pins when uploaded), every other point tethered to them; kept switchable ("Dress pinned at the shoulders", on), since the yardstick measures it held by contact too.
  - **Tethers along the fabric:** the straight line at rest was exact for every cloth before; across an armhole it is shorter than the fabric (70 cm up the pattern the line runs at x = 23.3 cm, where the fabric ends at 20.5), and a tether that short would pull the fabric tight. For a flat piece the exact distance is the shortest path inside its outline: a visibility graph over its reflex corners, Dijkstra from each anchor, once (`ClothRestPaths`, in place of `clothRestDistance`); no tether crosses a seam. `addClothPins` pins many points and rebuilds the tethers once, on every core.
  - **One test asked too much:** every point within its tethers failed by 1.1 cm at the middle of the back neckline, which rides up the back of the mannequin's neck: the straight line to the straps runs through the neck, the fabric lies taut round it, and the bodies, solved last, keep it out. A snug neckline, not a fault; the test now holds every point not touching the body to its tethers. The user, in the lab: "it looks great".
  - **On the GPU:** a stitch moves two points, so an invocation per point would read its partner while another writes it. Each point is in one stitch at most, so `cloth_stitch.comp` runs one invocation per stitch, giving both its points the hand and tethers the end's pass would have (`clothHandAndTethers`, which the end's pass now skips for them) and then the stitch: the CPU's order, one dispatch more a substep. The closing step went into the tick record's spare word. The user, in the lab with Cloth on GPU: "looks great".
